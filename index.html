Who funds whom in the AI buildout

A small number of companies are simultaneously each other's suppliers, customers, lenders, guarantors and shareholders. This is a map of that circuit — not of model capability. Every material figure carries a source tier, and the distinctions that most coverage collapses are held open throughout: committed capital is not a guarantee, a guarantee is not an option, and what a company reports as capex is a fraction of what it has committed to spend.

§0 — Method

Three distinctions, and a tier on every number

A large share of what circulates as AI capex data is aggregator commentary restating other aggregators. Two conventions run through this document and are worth reading before the figures.

Confidence tiering

A  Filing or direct disclosure

Company filing, press release or earnings disclosure. Treat as fact.

B  Wire or named research house

Reuters, Bloomberg, CNBC, FT, WSJ; or Moody's, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Barclays, Goldman, S&P, BIS, IEA. Reliable, but check for revision.

C  Secondary or conflicting

Aggregator, single-source trade press, or a figure that contradicts other reporting. Directional only. Do not chart without a caveat.

The three things wearing the same costume

Almost every headline number in this space is one of three different instruments, and getting them wrong is the single largest source of error in AI capex analysis.

Why the distinction is not academic

Nvidia's OpenAI Ohio arrangement was reported in late July 2026 as a roughly $250B guarantee. The 8-K filed on 17 August 2026 described a capped, conditional structure at $105B — a difference of about $145B between headline and contract, inside three weeks. A/B

Two naming conventions

Codenames are followed by what they name, because a codename on its own tells a reader nothing about what was bought: Blackwell (B200, B300), Rubin (Vera Rubin NVL144), Trainium (AWS), TPU (Google, Trillium v6e and Ironwood v7), MTIA (Meta), Maia (Microsoft), Colossus (xAI’s Memphis cluster), Stargate (the SoftBank, OpenAI, Oracle and MGX joint venture). And where a model is released openly, the entry says so and says which kind — open weights, meaning downloadable parameters without training code or data, is what Llama, DeepSeek and most of Mistral ship, and it is not open source. The distinction matters here for a financing reason rather than a licensing one: an open-weights release does not require the releasing lab to keep paying for inference, so it sits outside the offtake commitments this document tracks.

Scope. This covers the financing circuit, not model capability. Coverage spans foundry and lithography, memory, merchant and custom silicon, hyperscalers, neoclouds, frontier labs, data centre developers, power, and the full capital stack — corporate cash flow, investment-grade bonds, private credit, securitisation, vendor financing, venture, sovereign wealth, and government at federal, state and local level.

Non-dollar commitments — gigawatts, TPU counts, share-count warrants — sit on the graph and in the table but stay out of the arithmetic, and are marked n/a. Three further conventions apply and each one moves the totals. Equity positions are counted at what was paid, not at what they are now worth: Nvidia’s Intel stake enters at the disclosed ~$5B investment rather than its ~$30B mark, and the SpaceX and CoreWeave positions, whose cost bases have never been disclosed, are carried as announced and excluded from contracted. Contracts billed monthly are annualised rather than multiplied out to a soft end date, so Anthropic’s and Google’s Colossus (xAI’s Memphis cluster) agreements enter at roughly $15B and $11B a year; that makes them a one-year quantity sitting beside multi-year contract totals, which the term column exists to expose. Company-wide guidance is not a bilateral flow: ASML’s €43–45B revenue outlook is excluded entirely, since TSMC has never published what it actually ordered. The term column is the single most under-reported field in this space: a $300B cloud contract spread across five years and a $105B guarantee running twenty are not comparable quantities, and neither is an annual capex figure. Stake, share or price carries the ownership percentage, the coupon or the exercise price wherever one has been disclosed. Node size is not to scale; the graph is a topology, not a quantity chart. Where a figure has been superseded — Nvidia’s July Ohio report at ~$250B, the original $100B OpenAI announcement — the row carries the current contracted value and the readout names the earlier number.

Read the toggle, not the total

Switching to signed & committed only strips the MOU-stage platforms, the letters of intent and the unexercised warrants out of the graph — and takes roughly half the money with them. The headline circuit carries about $2.11T of named dollar figures; on a contracted basis it carries about $1.10T. The largest single line in the announced view, Nvidia's >$500B financing platform signed as MOUs across six managers on 10 August 2026, contributes nothing to the second number: no borrowers, rates, maturities or collateral have been disclosed. A Nvidia's Ohio guarantee behaves the same way — $250B as first reported, $105B as capped in the 8-K, and zero as capital deployed.

§2 — Deal grammar

Six archetypes cover every deal on the map

Once you can name the archetype, the headline number stops being surprising. Five of the six move capital in a direction that a casual reading gets backwards.

1  Equity-for-offtake

Supplier takes equity in the customer; the customer commits to buy from the supplier.

Nvidia → OpenAI. Up to $100B originally announced September 2025; $30B ultimately deployed within the $122B March 2026 round. B

2  Warrant-for-purchase — the reverse

The supplier gives the customer equity upside as an inducement to buy. The dilution falls on the supplier's existing shareholders.

AMD → OpenAI. Warrant for up to 160M AMD shares, about 10% of the company, vesting against 6GW of deployment and share-price milestones. A

Marvell → Google. 58,970,907 shares at $206.58 — roughly $12.2B at full exercise, about 7% of Marvell. One tranche vests per $500M of Google purchases through FY2033, against a potential ~$120B revenue ceiling. Google is obligated to buy nothing. A

3  Vendor guarantee

The supplier backstops the customer's lease or credit so the project can raise cheaper debt.

Nvidia → OpenAI / SB Energy Ohio. Residual value guaranties capped at $105B across about 4.25GW of IT load, triggered only by OpenAI default or insolvency, with OpenAI obliged to reimburse. The guarantee terminates once OpenAI achieves a satisfactory credit rating — which tells you exactly what it is for. A

4  Sponsored debt platform

The supplier convenes asset managers to lend to its own customer base, with the product as collateral.

Nvidia + Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, KKR. MOUs signed 10 August 2026 to mobilise more than $500B. Nvidia is not the lender; the six platforms underwrite independently. A

Broadcom + Apollo + Blackstone, "AI XPV". About $35B initial platform, June 2026, for roughly 1GW of Anthropic capacity; target above 20GW by 2028. In talks in August 2026 to raise $60–100B more. B

5  Prepaid offtake

The customer prepays to fund the supplier's build, inverting the working-capital burden.

Nebius. About 70% of signed deals carry customer prepayments; roughly $9B of prepayments in 2026 alone, covering 50–60% of capex on its landmark contracts. C

6  Sovereign co-investment

State capital takes equity in the asset and, implicitly, in the chip allocation.

AIP — BlackRock/GIP, MGX, Microsoft, Nvidia, later xAI — acquiring Aligned Data Centers at roughly $40B enterprise value, with Kuwait Investment Authority and Temasek as financial anchors. A

How the dollar disappears, in order

The question is not where the money went. It is which line of which statement each piece of it lands on, because only some of those lines reach a leverage ratio. Followed through the filings, a single campus decomposes like this.

  1  Sign the lease           4-year initial term, options out to ~20 years

                              no lease liability until they do

                              → purchase obligation, off balance sheet

Nothing in that sequence is concealment, and every step is audited and disclosed. The gap the $3T measures is between what a balance sheet is designed to answer, which is what an entity owes today, and what a reader of it is actually asking, which is what the company has committed to spend. Those were close enough to each other to ignore for most of the history of corporate reporting. They are not close now.

Read the word “hyperscaler” carefully

The $3T figure spans nine technology companies, not nine clouds: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Oracle, Nvidia, Broadcom, AMD and SpaceX. Three of those are chip vendors, whose purchase obligations are supply-chain commitments rather than data centre buildout, and two of the largest spenders in the set do not sell cloud in the sense the word implies. Meta has no public cloud at all, so none of its ~$420B of off-balance-sheet commitments amortises against external cloud revenue. SpaceX has been a compute landlord rather than a cloud since the February 2026 xAI merger, and spent $23.5B of its $28.4B of H1 2026 capex on AI. Both are covered at §3. Aggregates that describe this number as hyperscaler spending are measuring something broader than the phrase suggests, and the two names that stretch it furthest are also the two whose commitments have the least conventional revenue behind them.

Three mechanisms, and why each one works

1  Uncommenced leases

A multi-year data centre lease is signed, often with an initial term as short as four years plus renewal options, before the facility is operational. Under current accounting the lease frequently stays off balance sheet until it commences. Roughly $1.2T across the nine companies. B

2  Purchase obligations

Long-term commitments to buy GPUs, servers, memory or power remain off balance sheet until delivery. Roughly $1.9T, and the reason Alphabet’s disclosure is the largest single line in the dataset despite Meta having the largest lease book. B

3  SPVs, JVs and residual value guarantees

The structural layer. The operator builds and occupies; private credit owns; a bankruptcy-remote entity borrows against a single-tenant lease; and the operator’s guarantee — which is what makes the paper investment grade — appears only in a footnote. The synthesis

Four structures, one instrument. Meta guarantees a building. Broadcom guarantees a chip. Nvidia guarantees a lease — the $105B Ohio residual value guaranty in §2. Google’s guarantee network backs TPU-ecosystem operators’ borrowing costs, and J.P. Morgan’s $1.3B letter of credit does the same job for Volta in the Nvidia supply chain where Google’s reach does not extend.

The residual value guarantee is the load-bearing instrument of the entire buildout. §4C takes it apart mechanism by mechanism and totals what the vehicles shield; §4E totals the guarantees themselves, which four companies now disclose at roughly $217B. It is what converts an unrated tenant’s promise into investment-grade paper an insurance company can hold. It costs nothing until it costs everything. And in every case above, it lives in a footnote.

The trigger to watch

Not a default — a reclassification. Moody’s and S&P have both signalled they may fold uncommenced leases and SPV debt into adjusted leverage. That single decision would move a large share of the $3T onto reported credit metrics without a dollar changing hands or a single project failing. A second, quieter indicator is already moving: bond deal coverage ratios have fallen from about five times to under two times during 2026. C

Ten mechanisms that keep it in the footnotes

These are not loopholes in any pejorative sense. Each one is a defensible application of a real accounting standard. The effect, stacked, is that a $27.3 billion building financed by a $28 billion guarantee shows up in the lease tables as a $12.3 billion number.

+ 1 · The short initial lease term ASC 842-10-25-2

Hyperion’s leases are written as four-year non-cancellable blocks with renewal options that can take total occupancy to about twenty years. Four years is short for a hyperscale lease, where ten years is the usual floor. The brevity is the point: it keeps the lease in the operating bucket rather than the finance bucket, so rent is an ongoing operating expense rather than a capitalised liability.

To hold that classification, the tenant asserts two things. First, that renewal beyond the initial four years is not “reasonably certain”, and that four years is not a major part of a data centre’s 25-to-30-year economic life. Second, that the present value of four years of rent plus the amount guaranteed under the residual value guarantee is less than “substantially all” of the campus’s fair value — the so-called 90% test. Clear both and it is an operating lease. B

El Paso repeats it exactly: a four-year initial term with four options to extend, over a potential twenty-year term. A

The tension, stated by the people who write the bonds

Bondholders funding debt to 2049 are underwriting either a long series of renewals or a large residual cheque. The tenant is simultaneously asserting to its auditors that those renewals are not reasonably certain. Both statements can be true as accounting and underwriting propositions. They are difficult to hold as the same economic proposition. B

+ 2 · The residual value guarantee as a contingent liability the $28B footnote

This is the load-bearing mechanism, and Meta’s own 10-Q language is the clearest statement of it available anywhere. The disclosure says Meta provided a residual value guarantee for the first sixteen years of operations, under which it would make a capped cash payment to the venture if certain conditions are met following non-renewal or termination and the then-current value of the data centre falls below an agreed threshold — a threshold that effectively begins at $28 billion and decreases over time. The payment would be the difference between the then-current value and the threshold. A

The economics: if Meta declines to renew after four years, the venture sells the campus. If the sale yields less than the threshold, Meta pays the shortfall out of pocket. That is what convinces bondholders to fund twenty-four-year paper against four-year leases.

The accounting: because an RVG is legally a contingent liability, it is not recognised as a lease liability or as corporate debt until payment becomes probable. Until then it belongs in the commitments-and-contingencies footnote. If and when payment does become probable, the lease is remeasured to include the guaranteed amount — which is to say the $28B arrives on the balance sheet at precisely the moment it is least welcome. B

El Paso carries an aggregate RVG threshold of approximately $13 billion, also declining over time. Nvidia’s Ohio guaranty is the same instrument at a $105 billion cap, one layer up the stack. Broadcom’s deficiency guarantee is the same instrument applied to silicon. A

+ 3 · Minority equity and non-consolidation 20% / 80%

Meta takes 20% and the private-credit sponsor takes 80%. Meta’s 10-Q states the treatment plainly: its interest in the venture is accounted for as a non-marketable equity investment under the equity method. One line on the balance sheet, at cost. The venture’s $27.3 billion of debt does not consolidate, because Meta is not the primary beneficiary. A

Note what is genuinely transferred here and what is not. Blue Owl and BlackRock are taking real equity risk on 80% of the asset — this is not a circular self-dealing structure, and the off-balance-sheet critique applies to a minority of Hyperion’s true cost. What is not transferred is the demand risk, which the single-tenant lease and the RVG route straight back to Meta.

+ 4 · The uncommenced-lease exclusion $12.3B vs $27.3B

Hyperion’s leases commence in 2029. Until a lease commences there is no right-of-use asset and no lease liability, so nothing appears on the balance sheet at all. What appears instead is a disclosure: Meta’s initial lease commitment of approximately $12.3 billion for the four-year lease term. A

On the Enron comparison, which everyone reaches for

It cuts both ways and the honest reading needs both halves. Against: nobody is concealing these vehicles — Beignet and Sopaipilla are named in press releases and 10-Qs, the RVG thresholds are quantified, the ratings are public, and the sponsors are Blue Owl, BlackRock, PIMCO and Apollo rather than shell entities run by the CFO. S&P does not consolidate the structure and initially brings only eight years of lease payments into its base case, which is a disclosed and defensible analytical choice. For: the substance being routed around is economic incentive — a tenant asserting renewal is not reasonably certain while its bondholders are paid on the assumption that it is. One credit publication titled its analysis of the Meta—Blue Owl deal “off-balance-sheet gymnastics 24 years after Enron.” The distinction that matters is disclosure versus recognition, and on disclosure this cycle is genuinely better than 2001. B

What to actually do with this

Three practical adjustments follow, none of which require taking a view on whether AI demand materialises.

One. When benchmarking hyperscaler leverage, underwrite the uncommenced lease obligations as fixed claims rather than as options, and add the disclosed RVG thresholds as contingent debt. Reported debt materially understates infrastructure obligations at every name in §4B.

Two. Read the term and the exit, not the headline. A four-year lease with twenty years of options, a twenty-four-year bond and a sixteen-year declining guarantee are three different durations describing one asset, and the compute contracts in §5 include a $30B agreement cancellable on ninety days’ notice.

Three. Watch for reclassification, not default. If Moody’s and S&P fold uncommenced leases and SPV debt into adjusted leverage, a large share of the $3T in §4B moves onto reported credit metrics without a dollar moving or a single project failing.

Four different risks wearing one label

“Customer concentration” is used as though it means one thing. In this cycle it means four, and they fail in different ways and on different timetables.

1  Ordinary revenue concentration

The textbook version: one buyer leaves and the top line halves. Acute today rather than hypothetical, and the most visible. It is also the one the contracts are designed to answer — long duration, take-or-pay, prepayments at Nebius, twenty-year leases at TeraWulf.

The tell: a renewal option quietly not exercised. Microsoft passing on a $12B CoreWeave expansion in March 2025 was treated as a customer-specific adjustment rather than a structural signal. B

2  The customer becomes the competitor

The buyer builds the thing it was renting. Every hyperscaler is running multi-gigawatt custom silicon against Nvidia. Meta was reported on 1 July 2026 to be exploring commercialising excess AI capacity — Nebius and CoreWeave fell about 15% intraday, IREN about 6%. SpaceX already does exactly this, renting Colossus 1 to Anthropic and Google. Google second-sourcing TPU work to Marvell took 5% off Broadcom in a day.

The tell: your largest customer announcing a capacity marketplace. B

3  The customer is also the shareholder and the guarantor

This is the one with no textbook. Nvidia owns about 11% of CoreWeave, is simultaneously its largest supplier, and is contractually obliged to buy CoreWeave’s unsold capacity to 2032 — supplier, shareholder and buyer of last resort in one counterparty. §4E has the rest of the book. Nvidia is an anchor LP in the Valor vehicle that buys Nvidia GPUs and leases them to xAI. Google backstops Fluidstack’s TeraWulf leases with a $3.2B guarantee, activated $600M of credit support at Lake Mariner, and holds warrants that cost TeraWulf a $755.7M non-cash fair-value loss in Q2 2026 alone.

Why it matters: a demand problem at one counterparty arrives simultaneously as a revenue problem, an equity problem and a credit problem. The hedges are the same instrument. A

4  Look-through concentration — the diversification that isn’t

CoreWeave moving from 67% Microsoft to a spread of Microsoft, OpenAI, Meta and Anthropic is real diversification across counterparties and no diversification at all across risk: every one of those names is exposed to the same AI capex cycle. TeraWulf’s three customers resolve to two. Oracle’s backlog resolves to one.

The framing that matters: the question is no longer whether any operator depends on a single customer. It is whether that small, mutually-exposed group keeps expanding at the rate the valuations require. B

The counter-argument, which is not weak

Concentration is the normal opening condition of a platform cycle — rail, oil, software and cloud each began with a handful of buyers, and none of those concentrations proved fatal to the eventual winners. The contracts here are long, largely take-or-pay, and in Nebius’s case prepaid at 50–60% of capex. Marvell’s management has raised guidance twice against a fiscal 2029 custom revenue target above $10B, and its bulls argue the concentration story stopped matching the filings a year ago. Broadcom now has five disclosed XPU customers, which is broader than any peer. TeraWulf’s Fluidstack exposure carries a Google guarantee precisely because the counterparty risk was priced and then transferred. None of this is naive money.

What actually breaks, in what order

First, and already happening: renewal options quietly not exercised, and customers announcing they will resell capacity. Both are reversible and both have already produced double-digit single-day moves.

Second, 2027 onward: hyperscaler recapture as Trainium (AWS), TPU (Google, Trillium and Ironwood), MTIA (Meta) and Maia (Microsoft) scale. One analyst framing has neoclouds as tactical rather than strategic to their anchor customers. This is the slower risk and the larger one.

Third, the amplifier: leverage. Concentration alone is survivable; concentration financed with GPU-collateralised debt maturing 2026–28 into a correlated lender group is the combination that turns a customer decision into a credit event. That is why CoreWeave’s CDS moved before its equity did.

What would falsify the worry: a neocloud reaching a genuinely diversified enterprise book, a second tier-one hyperscaler win at Marvell that sticks, or an AI lab funding compute from operating cash flow rather than from capital raised against its own equity.

§4E — The backstop

When the tenant has no credit rating, the supplier signs for it

§4C explained how the debt leaves the balance sheet. This section is about what makes it bankable once it is out there. A backstop is a promise by a rated company to stand behind an obligation of a company that has no rating at all. It costs nothing on the day it is signed, it is disclosed as a contingency rather than as debt, and it is the reason an operator with negative free cash flow borrows at something close to investment grade. As of the most recent filings, four companies disclose roughly $217B of it.

+ The AI Compute Partnership, and the first retreat $36B committed, then paused

Announced on 1 July 2026, this was the demand floor turned into a product. Nvidia and the operator set a base hourly rate covering cost; Nvidia guarantees utilisation at that floor and rents back what does not sell; and Nvidia takes 50% of revenue above the base rate. The operator gets a revenue line a lender will underwrite. Nvidia gets the chip sale at full margin on day one and a share of the rental economics afterwards. B

At the Q2 FY2027 results on 26 August 2026 Nvidia disclosed the scale for the first time: $36B of commitments under agreements typically running six years, a figure management expects to shrink as the operators find their own customers. Then it stopped. Two retreats in one quarter

The Ohio guarantee was reported at about $250B in July and filed at a $105B cap in August. The revenue-share programme was launched in July and partly paused in August. Read together, these are the first evidence in this cycle of a supplier deciding that the marginal backstop costs more in scrutiny and disclosed contingency than it buys in chip demand. That is a limit arriving through the guarantor’s own caution rather than through a default, and it is the more likely way the practice gets bounded.

The part that is genuinely new

Vendor financing is old. Lucent and Nortel lent customers the money in 1999, and §7 treats that as the closest historical analogue. What is new here is the price: the guarantor is paid in equity of the party it is guaranteeing. Google took warrants over roughly 14% of TeraWulf and 5.4% of Cipher for standing behind a tenant that is neither. Nvidia took warrants in exchange for the $3.5B of facility guarantees. The backstop is therefore not a cost centre but a position.

Which creates a correlation no covenant addresses. The event that triggers the guarantee, a tenant failing, is the same event that makes the warrants worthless and the chips hard to remarket. The guarantor is long the asset, long the equity, and short the tail, all against the same handful of counterparties in §4D. Diversification of instrument is not diversification of risk.

What to watch

Three markers, none of which requires a default. Whether the escrow percentage behind new facility guarantees rises, since that is the cleanest available price on the risk. Whether the rating agencies begin treating disclosed guarantee caps as adjusted debt rather than as contingencies, the same reclassification flagged in §4B. And whether the paused revenue-share programme returns in a form that keeps the credit support and drops the revenue share, which would tell you the antitrust objection rather than the balance-sheet objection was the binding one.

§6 — Risk

Equity reprices enthusiasm in a week; lenders write covenants that last the loan

That asymmetry is the single most useful thing in this document. July 2026 supplied the demonstration.

Three assumptions the whole structure rests on

1  GPU residual value holds

If the economic life of an accelerator is three years rather than six, collateral values fall faster than loans amortise.

2  Offtake contracts are money-good

The senior tranches are investment grade because a vendor guarantee or a customer contract sits behind them. Both are only as strong as the counterparty — and there are very few counterparties. See §4D.

3  Refinancing is available, 2026–28

Tens of billions of GPU-collateralised loans mature in a narrow window, held by a small and correlated lender group.

What broke, and what didn't

What each side should concede

The bear should concede that revenue growth at Anthropic and the hyperscaler cloud units is not a projection — it is disclosed, audited, and compounding faster than almost any enterprise software cohort in history.

The bull should concede that the marginal financing structure has got visibly thinner: a seven-month-old company with a bank letter of credit standing in for a balance sheet is not the same risk as Microsoft's operating cash flow, and the market's willingness to fund the former is a function of conditions that can change quickly.

What would falsify each thesis

Bear thesis falsified if

  • OpenAI and Anthropic revenue compounds through 2027 at rates that close the Bain gap
  • GPU residual values hold in observable secondary transactions
  • The 2026–28 refinancing wall clears at spreads inside current levels

Bull thesis falsified if

  • A major neocloud misses a refinancing
  • A hyperscaler shortens GPU useful life materially and takes an impairment
  • An anchor offtake contract is renegotiated downward
